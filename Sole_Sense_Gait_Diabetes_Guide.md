## 1. What is gait?

**Gait** is the pattern or manner in which a person walks. It describes
how the legs, feet, joints, muscles, and nervous system work together to
move the body from one place to another.

Gait includes: - How the heel first touches the ground. - How body
weight moves across the foot. - How the toes push the body forward. -
How long each foot stays on the ground. - How the leg moves forward
through the air. - Walking speed, step length, rhythm, and balance.

In simple words: **gait is the way a person walks.**

## 2. What is a gait cycle?

A **gait cycle** is one complete walking cycle of one leg. It begins
when that foot contacts the ground and ends when the same foot contacts
the ground again.

For example: 1. The right heel touches the ground. 2. The right foot
supports the body. 3. The right toes push off. 4. The right foot moves
forward in the air. 5. The right heel touches the ground again.

The gait cycle is commonly divided into two main phases:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Phase                       Approximate percentage Meaning
  --------------------- ---------------------------- ---------------------
  Stance phase                                   60% The foot is in
                                                     contact with the
                                                     ground and supports
                                                     body weight.

  Swing phase                                    40% The foot is off the
                                                     ground and moves
                                                     forward.
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

These percentages are typical reference values, not fixed values for
every person or every walking speed.

### 2.1 Stance phase

The stance phase begins when the foot touches the ground and ends when
the toes leave the ground.

Its main purpose is to accept body weight, support the body, and help
move the body forward.

Common subdivisions:

1.  **Initial contact (heel strike):** The heel first touches the
    ground.
2.  **Loading response:** The foot comes down and accepts body weight.
3.  **Mid-stance:** The body moves over the supporting foot.
4.  **Terminal stance (heel rise):** The heel lifts while the forefoot
    remains on the ground.
5.  **Pre-swing (toe-off preparation):** Weight transfers to the other
    leg and the toes leave the ground.

### 2.2 Swing phase

The swing phase starts after toe-off. The foot is lifted and moves
forward to prepare for the next step.

Its subdivisions are:

1.  **Initial swing:** The foot lifts and starts moving forward.
2.  **Mid-swing:** The moving leg passes the supporting leg.
3.  **Terminal swing:** The leg slows down and prepares for the next
    heel contact.

## 3. Important gait terms

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Term                                Simple meaning
  ----------------------------------- -----------------------------------
  Step                                Movement from one foot contact to
                                      the next contact of the opposite
                                      foot.

  Stride                              One complete gait cycle of the same
                                      foot, such as right heel contact to
                                      the next right heel contact.

  Step length                         Distance between the contact point
                                      of one foot and the contact point
                                      of the opposite foot.

  Stride length                       Distance covered between two
                                      successive contacts of the same
                                      foot.

  Cadence                             Number of steps taken per minute.

  Walking speed                       Distance travelled per unit time.

  Stance time                         Time a foot remains in contact with
                                      the ground.

  Swing time                          Time a foot remains off the ground
                                      while moving forward.

  Double support                      Period when both feet are touching
                                      the ground.

  Single support                      Period when only one foot supports
                                      the body.

  Plantar pressure                    Pressure applied to the bottom
                                      surface (sole) of the foot.

  Centre of pressure (CoP)            The point representing the average
                                      location of the ground reaction
                                      force under the foot.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

**Remember:** A step is not the same as a stride. A stride includes two
steps in ordinary walking: one by each leg.

## 4. What is diabetes?

Diabetes is a long-term condition in which blood glucose (blood sugar)
levels are higher than normal. This happens when the body does not make
enough insulin or cannot use insulin effectively.

Insulin is a hormone that helps glucose move from the blood into cells
for energy.

Over many years, high blood glucose can damage nerves and blood vessels.
This can affect the feet and change how a person senses pressure, pain,
and movement.

Reference: - [NIDDK -- Diabetes
overview](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/what-is-diabetes)

## 5. What is diabetic neuropathy?

**Diabetic neuropathy** means nerve damage caused by diabetes. One
common type is peripheral neuropathy, which often affects the feet and
legs.

Possible symptoms include: - Numbness or reduced feeling. - Tingling or
a pins-and-needles sensation. - Burning or shooting pain. - Reduced
ability to feel pain or temperature. - Muscle weakness. - Problems
sensing the position or movement of the feet. - Changes in walking
pattern and balance.

A person may have reduced sensation and not notice a small stone,
blister, shoe rubbing, or injury.

Reference: - [NIDDK -- Peripheral
neuropathy](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/preventing-problems/nerve-damage-diabetic-neuropathies/peripheral-neuropathy) -

## 6. How are gait and diabetes connected?

Diabetes does not automatically cause an abnormal gait in every person.
However, diabetes-related nerve damage, reduced blood circulation, foot
deformity, pain, and muscle weakness can affect walking.

The main connections are explained below.

### 6.1 Nerve damage can change walking

1.  High blood glucose over time can damage peripheral nerves.
2.  The person may lose some sensation in the feet.
3.  They may not correctly sense contact, pressure, pain, or foot
    position.
4.  Their balance and walking pattern may change.

**Example:** A person may place their foot awkwardly because they cannot
sense the ground normally.

### 6.2 Abnormal foot pressure

The bottom of the foot is called the **plantar surface**.

During walking, different regions of the plantar surface experience
different pressure levels. These include: - Heel. - Midfoot. - Forefoot
(ball of the foot). - Toes.

Nerve damage and foot deformities can change the way pressure is
distributed. Some regions may experience repeated high pressure.

Repeated pressure, especially when combined with loss of protective
sensation and minor injury, can contribute to foot ulcers.

**Example:** A person with reduced sensation may repeatedly put high
pressure on one area without noticing irritation or skin damage.

### 6.3 Balance and coordination problems

Nerves provide information about foot position and movement. When this
information is reduced, a person may have difficulty maintaining
balance.

Possible effects: - Unsteady walking. - Shorter or slower steps. -
Increased variation in step timing. - Difficulty walking on uneven
surfaces. - Increased risk of falls.

### 6.4 Foot deformity and joint changes

Some people with diabetes develop changes in foot shape, toe position,
or joint structure. These changes can create pressure points.

A serious but less common complication is **Charcot
neuro-osteoarthropathy (Charcot foot)**, where bones and joints in the
foot can be damaged, often in the setting of neuropathy.

## 7. What is plantar pressure?

**Plantar pressure** is the pressure between the bottom of the foot and
the ground or footwear during standing and walking.

A pressure sensor measures force over a sensing area. Pressure is:

\[ P = `\frac{F}{A}`{=tex} \]

Where: - (P) = pressure. - (F) = force applied perpendicular to the
surface. - (A) = contact area.

The SI unit of pressure is pascal (Pa), equivalent to newton per square
metre (N/m²). Many plantar-pressure systems display kPa.

### Why measure plantar pressure?

It can help researchers and clinicians study: - Which areas of the foot
carry more load. - How pressure changes during a step. - Whether left
and right feet behave differently. - How long a region remains loaded. -
Whether pressure patterns change over time. - How footwear or orthotic
modifications affect loading.

**Important:** A pressure value alone does not diagnose an ulcer or
predict one with certainty. Clinical examination and the person's
medical history are also important.

## 8. How does Sole Sense work?

Sole Sense is a smart sandal concept that uses pressure sensors to
collect information about how a person loads their feet while standing
and walking.

The system can analyse pressure patterns and selected gait features.
Edge processing means that some or all of the analysis is performed on
the device itself rather than requiring every measurement to be sent to
a cloud server.

## 9. Where can pressure sensors be placed?

A simple prototype could begin with four sensing regions per foot:

  Sensor location   What it may help measure
  ----------------- ------------------------------------------
  Heel              Initial contact and heel loading.
  Midfoot           Midfoot contact and load distribution.
  Forefoot          Loading during late stance and push-off.
  Toe region        Toe loading and push-off activity.

A more detailed design may use multiple sensors under the heel,
metatarsal heads, big toe, and other toes.

**Design note:** Four sensors provide only a coarse pressure picture.
They do not measure the complete pressure distribution across the foot.
Sensor placement should be selected based on the research question,
footwear geometry, repeatability, and calibration.

### 10.3 Pressure-time integral

A simplified pressure-time integral is:

\[ PTI = `\int`{=tex}\_{t_0}\^{t_1} P(t),dt \]

It combines pressure magnitude and the time for which that pressure is
applied.

For sampled data, it can be approximated numerically:

\[ PTI `\approx `{=tex}`\sum`{=tex}\_{i=1}\^{N} P_i `\Delta `{=tex}t \]

The units depend on the pressure and time units used.

## 11. How can Edge AI be used?

**Edge AI** means running an artificial-intelligence model on or close
to the device that collects the data.

For Sole Sense, a model could analyse time-series pressure signals and
classify activities or identify changes in gait features.

Possible activity classes: - Standing. - Walking. - Running. - Sitting
or no foot loading. - Stair ascent/descent, if the system has enough
suitable sensors and training data.

Possible gait-related outputs: - Step events. - Stance and swing
timing. - Walking cadence. - Left-right pressure differences. - Changes
in pressure distribution. - Changes in walking pattern relative to a
person's baseline.

### Example processing pipeline

1.  Read sensor values at a consistent sampling rate.
2.  Apply sensor calibration.
3.  Filter noise without removing important step events.
4.  Detect foot contact and toe-off events.
5.  Divide the signal into steps or gait cycles.
6.  Extract features such as peak pressure and contact duration.
7.  Feed selected features or signal windows into a trained model.
8.  Display results and uncertainty to the user.

### Important distinction

An AI model should not simply label a person as diabetic from sandal
pressure readings. Diabetes diagnosis requires appropriate medical
testing. A wearable pressure system may support monitoring or research,
but clinical claims need validation with appropriate datasets, clinical
partners, and regulatory review.

## 12. How could Sole Sense support diabetic foot care?

A possible use case is longitudinal monitoring:

1.  A user wears the instrumented sandal during a defined walking test.
2.  The device records pressure signals from the foot.
3.  The system calculates features for each step.
4.  It compares results with the user's own baseline.
5.  It identifies persistent changes or unusual loading patterns.
6.  It may prompt the user to inspect their feet or seek advice from a
    healthcare professional.

Potential value: - Tracking changes in plantar loading. - Monitoring
walking patterns over time. - Supporting research into gait changes. -
Providing additional information to a clinician. - Encouraging foot-care
awareness.

**Safety limitation:** The device must not replace daily foot
inspection, clinical foot examinations, or medical advice. A person with
diabetes should not wait for a device alert if they notice a wound,
blister, redness, swelling, warmth, colour change, or other concerning
symptom.

The American Diabetes Association recommends comprehensive foot
evaluation at least annually, with more frequent assessment for people
at higher risk. Evaluation includes skin inspection, foot deformity
assessment, neurological assessment, and circulation assessment.

Reference: - [American Diabetes Association -- Standards of Care in
Diabetes, 2026, Section 12](https://doi.org/10.2337/dc26-s012)

## 14. Suggested prototype development plan

### Stage 1 -- Basic sensor test

-   Select pressure sensors.
-   Test each sensor under known loads.
-   Record repeatability, hysteresis, creep, and drift.
-   Determine useful sensing range.

### Stage 2 -- Mechanical sandal design

-   Design a comfortable and repeatable sensor mounting structure.
-   Place sensors in selected plantar regions.
-   Ensure wiring does not create discomfort or pressure points.
-   Test different foot sizes and footwear conditions.

### Stage 3 -- Embedded data acquisition

-   Connect sensors to suitable analogue front-end or ADC circuitry.
-   Read and timestamp samples.
-   Send data to a computer for initial visualisation.
-   Save raw data for analysis.

### Stage 4 -- Signal processing

-   Filter noise.
-   Calibrate sensor readings.
-   Detect contact and toe-off events.
-   Calculate gait features.
-   Compare left and right feet.

### Stage 5 -- Edge AI

-   Collect labelled data from a suitable participant group.
-   Train and validate activity-recognition models.
-   Test generalisation across people and walking speeds.
-   Deploy a compact model to the embedded device if appropriate.

### Stage 6 -- User interface and evaluation

-   Display pressure trends and gait features.
-   Show clear limitations and safety messages.
-   Evaluate usability, comfort, battery life, and measurement
    reliability.
-   Conduct clinical validation before making healthcare claims.

## 16. Key takeaways

1.  Gait means the way a person walks.
2.  A gait cycle is commonly divided into stance (about 60%) and swing
    (about 40%).
3.  Diabetes can damage nerves and blood vessels.
4.  Diabetic peripheral neuropathy can reduce foot sensation and affect
    balance and walking.
5.  Changes in foot structure and sensation can alter plantar pressure.
6.  Repeated pressure, especially with loss of protective sensation and
    minor trauma, can contribute to foot ulcers.
7.  Sole Sense can measure pressure patterns and analyse selected gait
    features.
8.  Edge AI can perform local activity or pattern analysis.
9.  The system is a monitoring/research aid, not a standalone diabetes
    or ulcer diagnostic device.
10. Sensor calibration, mechanical design, clinical validation, and user
    safety are essential.
